1. The Culture of Secrecy vs. The Right to Know

In a constitutional democracy, government officials are public servants, and public funds belong to the citizenry. Yet for decades following independence, bureaucratic functioning in India operated behind an impenetrable curtain of secrecy inherited from the colonial Official Secrets Act of 1923.

Constitutional Paradigm Shift: In State of U.P. v. Raj Narain (1975), the Supreme Court ruled that in a government of responsibility, the people have a constitutional right under Article 19(1)(a) to know every public act done by their public functionaries. This fundamental guarantee was codified into enforceable law via the Right to Information Act 2005.

2. Section 6 Drafting & Section 7 Mandatory Timelines

The Right to Information Act 2005 provides a structured statutory procedure to inspect files, take notes, and obtain certified copies of government records:

Section 6(1): Application Form

Plain English, Hindi, or state language. Nominal ₹10 application fee (BPL cardholders are 100% exempt from all fees under Sec 7(5)).

Section 6(2): No Reason Required

The applicant is legally NOT required to give any reason for seeking information or disclose personal details beyond contact address.

Section 7(1): 30-Day Limit

The Public Information Officer (PIO) must either provide information or reject with reasons within 30 calendar days of receipt.

Section 7(1) Proviso: 48-Hour Emergency

Where the information sought concerns the life or liberty of a person, the PIO is mandated to provide information within 48 hours.

Section 7(6) Free-of-Cost Penalty: If a public authority fails to provide information within the statutory 30-day (or 48-hour) limit, the information must subsequently be provided completely free of cost, regardless of the number of pages.

3. Narrow Section 8 Exemptions & The Public Interest Override

Public Information Officers frequently attempt to evade disclosure by citing Section 8 exemptions. However, the Supreme Court has strictly circumscribed these defenses:

CBSE & Anr. v. Aditya Bandopadhyay & Ors. (2011 8 SCC 497)
Supreme Court of India • Landmark RTI Jurisprudence

The Core Doctrine: The RTI Act is an indispensable democratic instrument. Exemptions in Section 8 must not be construed so broadly as to defeat the object of the Act. Public authorities cannot cloak routine records under vague claims of confidentiality.

  • Strict Fiduciary Test: An examining body or department holds answer keys, tender evaluations, and attendance records under public duty, not private commercial privilege.
  • Section 8(2) Public Interest Override: Even where a Section 8(1) exemption applies, the authority MUST disclose records if public interest in disclosure outweighs protected harm.
  • The Legislative Parity Test: Any information that cannot be denied to Parliament or a State Legislature cannot be denied to an everyday citizen.

4. Section 19 Appeals & Section 20 Personal Penalties on PIOs

When an application is ignored or unlawfully rejected, the statute arms citizens with a two-tier appellate mechanism and personal punitive remedies:

Section 19(1): First Appeal

Filed within 30 days of the PIO's refusal or expiry of 30 days to an officer senior in rank (First Appellate Authority) within the department.

Section 19(3): Second Appeal

Filed within 90 days before the Central Information Commission (CIC) or State Information Commission (SIC) against the FAA order.

Section 20(1): ₹250/Day Personal Fine

Commission imposes a fine of ₹250 per day up to a maximum of ₹25,000 deducted directly from the defaulting PIO's personal salary.

Section 20(2): Disciplinary Action

Commission can recommend disciplinary inquiry under service conduct rules against a PIO guilty of persistent obstruction.

Segment 1: The Culture of Secrecy vs. Right to Know

In a constitutional democracy, government officials are public servants, and public funds belong to the citizenry. Yet for decades following independence, bureaucratic functioning in India operated behind an impenetrable curtain of secrecy inherited from the colonial Official Secrets Act of 1923. Citizens demanding to know why a road project remained incomplete, how municipal funds were allocated, or why a passport application stalled for months were routinely dismissed without explanation. In 1975, the Supreme Court of India fundamentally dismantled this culture of administrative arrogance in the landmark ruling State of Uttar Pradesh versus Raj Narain. The Supreme Court established that in a government of responsibility where all agents of the public must be responsible for their conduct, the people have a constitutional right to know every public act done by their public functionaries. This constitutional principle under Article 19(1)(a) was codified into operational law with the enactment of the Right to Information Act of 2005. Today, you will master how to wield this statutory weapon to compel government transparency and hold defaulting bureaucrats personally accountable.

Segment 2: Section 6 Drafting & Section 7 Timelines

The Right to Information Act provides an accessible, non-technical mechanism for any citizen to inspect public records, obtain certified copies of contracts and logbooks, and request certified samples of public construction materials. Under Section 6(1) of the Act, an application can be drafted in plain English, Hindi, or the local official language, accompanied by a nominal fee of ten rupees. Crucially, under Section 6(2), the applicant is legally not required to give any reason for requesting the information or provide any personal details beyond what is necessary to contact them. Section 7 governs mandatory response timelines. The Public Information Officer, or PIO, must either provide the information or reject the request within thirty calendar days. However, the statute contains a vital emergency safeguard: under the express proviso to Section 7(1), where the information sought concerns the life or liberty of a person, the PIO is legally mandated to provide the information within forty-eight hours. If the department fails to respond within these statutory limits, Section 7(6) mandates that the information must subsequently be provided completely free of cost.

Segment 3: Section 8 Exemptions & Public Interest Override

Public authorities frequently attempt to evade disclosure by citing the exemptions listed in Section 8 of the Act. However, Indian jurisprudence strictly limits these exemptions. In CBSE versus Aditya Bandopadhyay, the Supreme Court established that the RTI Act must not be rendered a paper tiger; exemptions under Section 8 must be strictly and narrowly construed. A PIO cannot simply claim that an administrative file is confidential or internal. Commercial confidence under Section 8(1)(d) or fiduciary capacity under Section 8(1)(e) cannot be used to conceal illegal tender awards, diversion of welfare subsidies, or administrative wrongdoing. Most powerfully, Section 8(2) creates a statutory public interest override: even if an exemption under Section 8(1) applies, a public authority must disclose the information if the public interest in disclosure outweighs the harm to the protected interests. Furthermore, information that cannot be denied to Parliament or a State Legislature shall not be denied to any citizen.

Segment 4: Section 19 Appeals & Section 20 PIO Penalties

Consider Sunita, an activist in Pune who noticed that funds allocated for building a neighborhood maternity primary health center had been disbursed, but no construction had begun after two years. She submitted a Section 6 RTI application demanding contractor tenders, engineer completion certificates, and billing vouchers. The Public Information Officer ignored the request for sixty days. Sunita immediately filed a First Appeal under Section 19(1) before the senior departmental officer. When the appellate officer upheld the delay, Sunita escalated to the State Information Commission under Section 19(3). The Information Commission invoked Section 20 of the Act. Section 20 empowers the Commission to impose a personal, non-reimbursable fine of two hundred and fifty rupees per day, up to a maximum of twenty-five thousand rupees, directly deducted from the defaulting PIO's personal salary. Faced with personal financial liability and disciplinary sanctions under Section 20(2), the PIO delivered certified copies within seven days, exposing a multi-crore embezzlement scheme that was referred to the vigilance bureau.

Segment 5: The Citizen 4-Step RTI Action Playbook

Whenever you seek public accountability from a municipal corporation, university, police station, or government ministry, execute this four-step statutory playbook. Step One: Draft precise, numbered Section 6 queries. Request specific, existing documentary records—such as tender documents, attendance registers, inspection reports, and sanctioned budgets—rather than asking vague hypothetical questions. Step Two: File with the designated PIO. Submit your application online through rtionline.gov.in or in person with the statutory ten-rupee fee, obtaining an acknowledgment receipt with date and stamp. If the matter involves illegal custody, emergency healthcare, or immediate safety, invoke the Section 7(1) forty-eight-hour life and liberty proviso. Step Three: File a First Appeal within thirty days. If thirty days elapse without response, or if information is unlawfully denied, file an appeal under Section 19(1) to the First Appellate Authority. Step Four: Escalate to the Information Commission. If the First Appeal fails, file a Second Appeal under Section 19(3) demanding Section 20 personal daily penalties against the officer.
